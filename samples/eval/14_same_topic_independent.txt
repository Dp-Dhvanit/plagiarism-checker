Cities have always run a few degrees warmer than the land surrounding them, but the
gap has widened as construction has intensified. Engineers call this the urban heat
island effect, and its footprint shows up clearly in satellite thermal imagery taken
after sunset.

The mechanism is straightforward thermodynamics. Concrete and asphalt have a much
higher heat capacity than soil or vegetation, so they soak up solar radiation during
the day and release it slowly through the night. A tree canopy, by contrast, cools
the air through evapotranspiration and casts shade that never lets pavement reach
peak temperature in the first place.

Municipal planners in Phoenix and Singapore have both experimented with reflective
"cool pavement" coatings that bounce sunlight back into the atmosphere rather than
absorbing it. Early results are mixed: the coatings lower surface temperature but can
increase glare and, in some studies, raise the ambient temperature felt by pedestrians
at eye level even as the road surface itself cools.

A more consistent finding across dozens of cities is that tree cover correlates with
lower nighttime temperatures far more reliably than any single engineered surface
treatment. That has pushed several city governments to fund canopy expansion as a
public-health measure rather than a purely aesthetic one, tying tree-planting budgets
directly to emergency-room admissions during summer heat waves.
